## Step 0 — What diversification is for

Diversifying means avoiding putting your fate on a single bet. The idea is simple: if all your capital depends on one asset and it drops, you have no safety net.

What diversification aims to reduce:

- **specific risk** (a company, a sector, an issuer going wrong)
- **geographic risk** (a single region in trouble)
- **timing risk** (buying everything at the worst moment)

What it doesn't remove:

- **market risk** (a broad decline hits almost everything)
- **behavioral mistakes** (panicking, over-trading)

## Step 1 — Diversify by asset class

Spread across several types of assets that don't react the same way:

- **stocks** (growth, more volatile)
- **bonds** (often more stable, sensitive to rates)
- **cash** (safety, room to maneuver)
- **real estate / real assets** (via suitable vehicles)
- **crypto** (high volatility, to be dosed carefully)

The goal isn't to have some of everything, but to have building blocks whose behaviors differ.

## Step 2 — Diversify by geography

A portfolio concentrated in a single country is exposed to its economy, its currency, and its politics.

- spread across several regions (developed and emerging markets)
- keep currency risk in mind if you invest outside your currency
- a global index fund already covers broad geographic diversification

## Step 3 — Diversify by sector and size

Within stocks, avoid betting everything on the same theme:

- **sectors** (tech, healthcare, energy, consumer…)
- **company size** (large caps vs smaller ones)
- **style** (growth vs value)

A portfolio dominated by a single sector trend is fragile when that trend fades.

## Step 4 — Diversify over time

Buying all at once exposes you to the risk of poor timing. Spreading purchases smooths the entry price:

- **DCA**: invest a fixed amount at regular intervals
- reduces the impact of bad timing
- makes the routine more sustainable emotionally

## Step 5 — Limits of diversification

- it **doesn't protect** against a general market decline
- beyond a certain point, adding holdings **no longer brings** risk reduction
- too many products = more fees and harder monitoring
- diversification "on paper" can hide redundancies (two funds holding the same stocks)

## Step 6 — Rebalancing

Over time, weights drift: a holding that rises takes up too much space.

- define a **target allocation** (e.g. X% stocks, Y% bonds)
- rebalance **by calendar** (1–2 times/year) or **by threshold** (excessive drift)
- watch out for fees and taxes tied to trades

Rebalancing forces you to sell what has risen and reinforce what has fallen, which disciplines management.

## Example structure

Purely for illustration, a portfolio can combine:

- a broad **global stocks** base (geo + sector diversification)
- a **bonds / cash** pocket to cushion
- a small **crypto** pocket if the risk profile allows
- purchases **spread over time** rather than all at once

The proportions depend on your horizon and risk tolerance, not on a universal formula.

## Checklist

- [ ] I've spread across several **asset classes**
- [ ] I have **geographic** diversification
- [ ] I'm not concentrated in a single **sector**
- [ ] I **spread my purchases** over time
- [ ] I have a **target allocation** and a rebalancing plan
- [ ] I've checked there are no **redundancies** between my holdings

## Common mistakes

- **confusing number of holdings with diversification**: ten identical funds diversify nothing
- **forgetting currency risk** on assets in foreign currency
- **never rebalancing** and letting one holding dominate the whole portfolio
- **over-diversifying** to the point of no longer following or understanding anything
- **overweighting a trendy theme** right after a strong rally
