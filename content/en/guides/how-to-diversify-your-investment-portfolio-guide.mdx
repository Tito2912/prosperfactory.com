## Why diversification matters

Diversification is spreading your money so that no single position, sector, or event can wreck the whole portfolio. The goal isn’t to maximize returns on every bet—it’s to reduce the damage when one bet goes wrong.

A useful way to think about it:

- **Concentration** rewards being right and punishes being wrong—hard.
- **Diversification** smooths outcomes by combining holdings that don’t all move the same way at the same time.

The key idea is **correlation**. Two holdings that rise and fall together add little diversification, even if they’re “different” on paper.

## Dimension 1 — Asset classes

Different asset classes tend to react differently to the same events. Common building blocks:

- **Equities:** growth potential, higher volatility.
- **Bonds / fixed income:** typically steadier, income-focused.
- **Cash / cash-like:** stability and liquidity for flexibility.
- **Crypto:** high volatility—treat any allocation as high-risk.

Mixing classes with different behaviors is usually more effective than adding more of one class.

## Dimension 2 — Geography

Countries and regions don’t always move together. A portfolio concentrated in one market carries the risk of that market’s economy, currency, and politics.

- Spread exposure across regions where it makes sense for you.
- Note the **currency** angle: foreign holdings add FX risk (and potential FX fees).
- Avoid assuming “global” automatically means balanced—some funds are heavily weighted to one country.

## Dimension 3 — Sectors

Within equities, sectors behave differently across the economic cycle.

- A portfolio loaded with one sector rises and falls with that sector.
- Check whether your holdings quietly overlap in the same theme (for example, several positions all tied to one industry).
- Broad exposure across sectors reduces the impact of any single one struggling.

## Dimension 4 — Time

Diversifying *when* you invest matters too.

- Investing gradually over time (instead of one large lump) spreads entry-price risk.
- A recurring, rules-based approach removes some emotion and timing pressure.
- Time diversification pairs well with a long horizon and a routine you can keep.

## The limits of diversification

Diversification is powerful, but it isn’t a shield against everything:

- **It doesn’t remove market-wide risk.** In broad downturns, most assets can fall together.
- **More holdings ≠ more diversified.** Overlap hides behind large numbers.
- **Over-diversification** can dilute results and add complexity you can’t manage.
- **It doesn’t guarantee profit** or prevent loss.

Complexity itself is a risk: a portfolio you can’t explain or maintain is harder to manage safely.

## Rebalancing

Over time, winners grow and your weights drift away from your plan. Rebalancing brings them back to target.

- Set **target weights** (for example, per asset class).
- Choose a **trigger**: a fixed schedule (once or twice a year) or a drift threshold.
- Mind **costs and taxes** when you rebalance—frequent adjustments add friction.

The best rebalancing rule is the one you’ll actually follow.

## A simple starter approach

If you’re early on, keep it manageable:

1. Decide your risk tolerance and time horizon.
2. Pick a small number of broad, low-overlap building blocks.
3. Set target weights you can explain in one sentence.
4. Invest on a routine rather than reacting to headlines.
5. Review and rebalance on a fixed schedule.

You can refine over time—starting simple beats never starting.

## Checklist

- Can you name your main asset classes and their rough weights?
- Are your holdings spread across geography and sectors, or secretly overlapping?
- Do you understand each position and why it’s there?
- Have you set target weights and a rebalancing trigger?
- Is any high-risk allocation (like crypto) sized so a big drop won’t derail your plan?
- Is the whole setup simple enough for you to maintain?

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing “more holdings” with diversification** when everything overlaps.
- **Home bias:** concentrating in a single country or currency.
- **Chasing last year’s winners** and crowding into one theme.
- **Ignoring costs and FX** that quietly erode returns.
- **Over-diversifying** into a portfolio too complex to manage.
- **Never rebalancing**, letting a few winners dominate your risk.
